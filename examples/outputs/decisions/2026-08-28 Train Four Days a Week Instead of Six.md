# Train Four Days a Week Instead of Six

## Context

Week 7 of the plan. Six sessions a week was working on paper and failing in practice:
two sessions a week were being skipped anyway, and the left calf tightened after every
back-to-back pair. The choice was between defending the plan and changing it.

## Options

| Option | For | Against |
|---|---|---|
| Keep six days | Matches the published plan; more total volume | Two days were being missed regardless; injury risk was real and recurring |
| Drop to four days | Every session becomes non-optional; two easy days between hard days | Less total volume; feels like giving up |
| Keep six, reduce intensity | Preserves volume | Does not fix the back-to-back problem, which was the actual cause |

## Decision

Four days a week, at fixed times, with hard days never adjacent.

The reason that actually decided it: I was already training four days a week. The
difference was only whether I was doing it on purpose.

## Expected outcome

Adherence above 90% over the next four weeks, and no calf tightness. If mileage
stalls below 16 km by late September, this was the wrong call and I add a fifth day.

## Outcome

Reviewed 2026-09-11. Four weeks at 100% adherence, longest run up to 14 km, no calf
symptoms. Volume ended up *higher* than under the six-day plan because nothing was
being skipped. Marked `good`. The general lesson — that a lower target can raise the
outcome when the constraint is decision-making — is compiled into
[[2026-08-20 Notes on Habit Formation]] and cited by [[Progressive Overload]].
